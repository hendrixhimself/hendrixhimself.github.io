~ TOPIC OVERVIEW ~

⚠ EDUCATIONAL CONTENT AHEAD ⚠

What is digital piracy?

It means sharing copyrighted media (movies, music, books, games, etc.) without permission via the Internet. Classic methods include torrenting or direct downloads, but now streaming or mobile apps often offer illegal content too. For example, as of 2023 there were over 229 billion annual visits to piracy websites worldwide (MUSO, 2023). Digital pirates include individuals downloading or uploading, and organized groups running illegal streaming platforms.

Who is involved?

On the consumer side, studies find millions of Internet users, especially younger people, access pirated media. For instance, one report notes that Gen Z and millennials account for over 70% of piracy site traffic (ElectroIQ). On the supplier side, piracy is often run by international networks. An EU police operation (Kratos 2, 2026) took down nine organized crime groups behind sports and media streaming sites (Europol). Even social media can be a factor, with some users reposting copyrighted videos or music on TikTok or Instagram.

Where and When?

Piracy is global. High-income countries (US, UK, Germany) tend to see billions of visits, but rapidly developing markets (India, Brazil, Indonesia) have shown the fastest growth. By MUSO’s count, the U.S., Russia, Brazil, and India led piracy traffic in 2018 (MUSO). Trends by year: piracy visits were roughly 190B in 2018, dipped slightly, and then jumped to 182B in 2021, 215B in 2022, and 229B in 2023. An EUIPO report noted a 3.3% rise in piracy in 2022 after years of decline (EUIPO, 2023). In Europe, people now average about 10 accesses of pirated material per month.

Why is piracy increasing now?

Several modern day factors contribute. One is the phenomena of streaming fragmentation, with Netflix, Disney+, HBO Max and many others launching (especially in 2018‐2022), content became scattered under several different paywalls. Research indicates many users turn to piracy when legal access is complicated or costly, which legal streaming has currently amounted to both. For example, the American Entertainment Association reported users justifying piracy by “the explosion of streaming options” and rising subscription costs. The advancement of technology also helps pirates, with easy-to-use VPNs and streaming devices allowing hidden access to illegal streams. Social norms play a role too, with some viewing piracy-streaming as acceptable or low-risk, as opposed to torrenting.

How does piracy happen?

In technical terms, common methods include peer-to-peer (BitTorrent) file sharing, streaming from illegal sites or apps, and “stream-ripping” (downloading audio from YouTube and similar sites). MUSO data shows streaming sites are now the dominant piracy method:, with about 60% of piracy traffic coming from unlicensed web streaming (MUSO). For music, stream-ripping sites accounted for 39% of piracy in 2021, reflecting a shift from downloading MP3s to ripping from video sites (Music Business Worldwide, 2021).

In summary, digital piracy has surged recently due to a mix of economic pressures (high subscription fees), improved technology (VPNs, streaming ports), and global demand. For in-depth industry data, see the MUSO Insights research hub.

~ MASS MEDIA COVERAGE ~

The media’s treatment of digital piracy spans news stories, films/TV, books, podcasts, and even music. Below are examples from different formats illustrating how piracy is portrayed or critiqued:

1. Newspaper – Film leak (The Independent)

Major news outlets report on high-profile leaks. The Independent covered how Avengers: Endgame (2019) was illicitly filmed and shared online before its U.S. premiere (The Independent). These stories show how blockbusters are immediately targeted by pirates and often spark industry calls for anti-piracy measures.

2. Trade press / Magazine – TV piracy (Variety)

Trade press like Variety discusses top pirated titles, noting Spider-Man: No Way Home and House of the Dragon among the most-streamed illegally (Variety).

3. Internet / Gaming media – ROM site raid (Kotaku)

Kotaku detailed a July 2025 FBI operation that seized NSW2U, a popular Nintendo Switch ROM site responsible for roughly 36 million visits and an estimated $170 million in lost sales (Kotaku).

4. Video games – Pre-release leaks

Tech and gaming news cover when new games or consoles are leaked early, such as reports of GTA VI pre-release leaks and rumors of console bans for pirating on Switch 2.

5. Internet / Technology – Anti-piracy lobbying (TechSpot)

TechSpot reported in 2024 that Hollywood studios and the Motion Picture Association were lobbying the U.S. Congress for new anti-piracy laws targeting illegal streaming sites (TechSpot).

6. Internet – Industry coalition (ACE / TorrentFreak)

The Alliance for Creativity and Entertainment regularly announces takedowns in Europe and Asia; tech blogs such as TorrentFreak publish these industry press releases.

7. Television / Sports media – Live stream raids

Authorities recently estimated nearly 850,000 pirated sports event links shut down by a Europol-led raid, Operation Kratos 2 (Europol, 2026).

8. Books & Academic journalism – EUIPO studies

Academic press releases and articles (e.g., EUIPO’s 2023 report on copyright infringement) are covered by news outlets. Investigative journalism has also profiled famous pirates such as Kim Dotcom and Megaupload.

9. Music industry trade press – Rising music piracy

Music Business Worldwide highlighted that after a period of decline, online music piracy began rising again in 2021 (Music Business Worldwide). The RIAA also regularly issues news on shutting down stream-ripping sites.

10. Radio / Podcast – DoveRunner Cybersecurity (Feb 2026)

The DoveRunner Cybersecurity podcast devoted an episode to piracy, noting how it has evolved into an organized global operation causing roughly 15–30% revenue losses (DoveRunner).

11. Internet / Video – YouTube commentary

Tech commentators on YouTube debate piracy trends; one popular framing is “The Streaming War Is Over. Piracy Won,” analyzing how consumer frustration led to widespread pirate streaming.

12. Internet / Social media – Reddit and enforcement news

Reddit’s r/piracy has roughly 1.2M members discussing justifications for piracy. In early 2023, UK authorities announced police visits to thousands of alleged illegal stream watchers, a story covered widely in the press (The Independent / FACT report).

Collectively, these examples show that the press tracks big piracy stories (movie leaks, game ROM seizures) and the industry responds with newsworthy enforcement actions.
